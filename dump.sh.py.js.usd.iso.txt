Here is the operational framework and legal sequence for executing property seizure through the court system:
Phase 1: Judgment Acquisition
Before any asset can be seized, a formal judgment must be entered by a court declaring that the debtor legally owes a specific amount of money.
Phase 2: Asset Discovery
Locating the debtor's attachable assets (bank accounts, real estate, vehicles, or business inventory). If assets are hidden, courts can issue:
* Interrogatories/Depositions: Forcing the debtor to answer questions regarding their finances under oath.
* Subpoenas: Demanding financial records from third parties (e.g., banks, employers, or business partners).
Phase 3: Execution Instruments
Once assets are identified, specific legal mechanisms are filed with the court to initiate the seizure:
* Writ of Execution: A court order directing local law enforcement (usually a sheriff or U.S. Marshal) to physically seize the debtor's property.
* Bank Garnishment (Non-Wage Garnishment): An order served directly to the debtor's bank, compelling the institution to freeze the account and surrender the available funds up to the judgment amount.
* Wage Garnishment: A directive sent to the debtor's employer requiring them to withhold a legal percentage of the debtor's earnings and remit it to the court or creditor.
* Real Estate Liens: A legal claim attached to the debtor's property title. While it does not immediately evict the debtor, the property cannot be refinanced or sold without first paying the judgment out of the proceeds.
* Till Tap / Keeper Levy: Specific to businesses, law enforcement is dispatched to physically empty the business's cash register or sit on-site to collect incoming revenue for the day.
Phase 4: Asset Liquidation
If physical property (like a vehicle or equipment) is seized via a Writ of Execution, law enforcement will typically hold a public auction (Sheriff's Sale). The proceeds from the liquidation, minus administrative and storage fees, are applied to the judgment balance.
